fire [1]. Statistical data shows that the number of casualties in subway fire incidents has been on the rise, with the period from

Timely and effective firefighting strategies are crucial for minimizing losses in the event of a fire. Subway station staff should

effectiveness of firefighting operations. Traditional firefighting strategies may struggle to address the complexities of fire propagation
and suppression in subway station environments, with poorly planned rescue efforts potentially exacerbating the difficulty and
severity of incidents. Therefore, developing a firefighting route that can effectively respond to fire propagation while efficiently

The development of firefighting routes for subway station staff faces numerous challenges, including complex station structures,
narrow passageways, smoke dispersion, and temperature variations under uncertain conditions. Traditional firefighting strategies
often fail to address the uncertainties of sudden fires, rendering them ineffective when the environment changes. To address this
issue, this study fully considers the impact of uncertainties such as fire propagation, smoke dispersion, and temporal changes, and
proposes a robust optimization model for the firefighting route. This model ensures that the developed firefighting strategies remain
feasible and effective under various possible scenarios, thereby reducing the risk of failure caused by environmental changes.
The main contributions of this study are summarized as follows: (1) A core objective function is constructed, incorporating
firefighting value, travel distance, and human resource expenditure, with uncertainty modeling applied to travel time and firefighting
time. This enhances the applicability and robustness of firefighting route solutions in complex real-world scenarios. (2) A BKA-GRU
prediction model is designed to accurately estimate the time required for personnel to pass through gates and stairs, providing
reliable data support for solving firefighting routes. (3) The feasibility and safety of the robust optimization model for the firefighting
route are validated through the integration of the PyroSim fire simulator and PathFinder pedestrian dynamics simulator. (4) By
quantitatively analyzing route distance, evacuation risks, and congestion factors, the reliability and practical value of the firefighting
optimization strategy are further verified.
2. Related research
2.1. Fire numerical simulation
Numerical simulation of fires is an important tool in fire prevention research, enabling comprehensive analysis of fire scenario
evolution by simulating dynamic processes such as smoke dispersion, heat transfer, combustion, and personnel evacuation. In densely
populated subway stations, fires not only threaten the normal functions of station facilities but also significantly impact the safety
and efficiency of passenger evacuation. Fire numerical simulation technology allows for the virtual simulation of fire evolution,
providing insights into how various fire conditions and firefighting strategies influence passenger evacuation behavior, thereby
offering scientific guidance for fire prevention. Currently, mainstream software tools for fire numerical simulations include fire
dynamics simulator (FDS) [3], PyroSim [4], and Fluent [5], each with its unique features and applications. These tools provide
reliable and scientific solutions for fire modeling.
FDS, based on computational fluid dynamics (CFD) theory, enables precise simulation of smoke dispersion, temperature fields,
and combustion reactions during fires. It has been widely used for modeling fire evolution in large structures such as subway
stations [6]. For example, Tsukahara et al. [7] utilized FDS to simulate the distribution of smoke, temperature, and toxic gases,
analyzing the effectiveness of large-scale evacuation in subway fire scenarios, with a particular focus on the advantages of downward
evacuation. PyroSim, a graphical user interface software built on FDS, simplifies the creation, editing, and analysis of FDS models.
Its visual interface makes fire scenario evolution more intuitive and has been widely adopted [8]. For instance, Yu et al. [9] used
PyroSim to assess fire evolution risks at a subway station and optimize the design of its smoke extraction system. Fluent, a general-
purpose CFD software, is applicable to a broad range of simulations, including fluid flow, heat transfer, chemical reactions, and
multiphase flows, extending beyond fire simulation applications [10].
FDS is a software specifically developed for fire dynamics simulation, and PyroSim inherits its professional capabilities. PyroSim’s
graphical interface simplifies the creation of complex geometric models and parameter settings for subway stations, thereby
significantly improving modeling efficiency. Additionally, PyroSim seamlessly integrates with PathFinder, a pedestrian dynamics
simulation tool, allowing for simultaneous analysis of fire dynamics and evacuation behaviors. This integration is critical for fire
prevention and control research in densely populated spaces such as subway stations. Therefore, this study adopts PyroSim as the
numerical simulation tool for fire scenarios.

2.2. Pedestrian evacuation simulation
In the context of subway station fires, passenger evacuation efficiency not only affects post-disaster response speed but also
directly relates to the safety of lives. Pedestrian evacuation simulation is a critical method for analyzing crowd dynamics and
assessing evacuation efficiency, widely applied in building design, safety management, and emergency evacuation planning [11].
Currently, pedestrian evacuation models can be categorized into two main types, namely macroscopic models and microscopic
models [12]. The predominant macroscopic models are primarily based on fluid dynamics [13], while microscopic models mainly
include social force models, rule-based cellular automata models, and agent-based models [14–16].
Fluid dynamics models primarily treat crowds as continuous media, focusing on the relationships between crowd density,
speed, and flow. These models excel at providing an overall analysis of large-scale crowd movement. However, they cannot
capture the nuanced differences in individual behaviors, which presents limitations in addressing complex interactions in dynamic
environments. Given the need for firefighting route optimization during subway station fires as studied in this paper, the applicability
of macroscopic models is constrained.
Microscopic models focus on simulating the behavioral characteristics of individual pedestrians, allowing them to capture
dynamic changes in individuals’ behaviors under different scenarios. The social force model, one of the most commonly used
microscopic models, simulates interactions between pedestrians and demonstrates individual behavioral variations under different
environmental factors. It has been applied to simulate passenger boarding and alighting behaviors [17], evacuation behaviors [18–
20], and group travel behaviors [21]. Cellular automata models discretize space and simulate pedestrian movement through simple
rules, making them widely used for flow optimization [22] and pedestrian flow analysis [23]. However, the spatial discretization
of cellular automata models limits movement directions, making it challenging to simulate the complex decision-making behaviors
of passengers. Agent-based models adopt continuous spatial modeling, treating each pedestrian as an independent agent capable of
making personalized decisions based on attributes such as age, gender, walking speed, and environmental conditions. This model has
been applied to analyze evacuation behaviors [24], predict passenger flow distribution [25], and simulate emergency responses [26].
Subway stations often feature complex geometrical structures, including platforms, stairs, gates, and corridors. The continuous spatial
modeling capabilities of agent-based models can accurately describe how these structures influence passenger evacuation.
With the increasing complexity of research scenarios and the heightened demand for simulation accuracy, pedestrian dynamics
simulation software has emerged. By integrating pedestrian evacuation models with computer simulation techniques, these tools
enable efficient and precise simulation of pedestrian groups in complex environments. Currently, widely used pedestrian dynamics
simulation software includes PathFinder [27–29], MassMotion [30], and Legion [31], which incorporate agent-based models, social
force models, and cellular automata models, respectively to simulate pedestrian movements. Considering the characteristics of the
pedestrian evacuation model utilized by PathFinder, as well as its capability to directly import dynamic data such as smoke and
temperature from PyroSim into the evacuation model, this study employs PathFinder to simulate passenger evacuation behaviors
during a fire, thereby enhancing the realism and reliability of the simulation [32].
2.3. Firefighting route optimization
As densely populated public spaces, subway stations present complex challenges during fires, including rapid smoke dispersion,
reduced visibility, and passenger panic. These factors can significantly impact the efficiency of firefighting personnel’s response.
Optimizing firefighting routes ensures that firefighters can reach the fire scene in the shortest time and safest manner, effectively
controlling the spread of the fire and providing valuable time for passenger evacuation. For instance, the selection of firefighting
routes can influence the speed and direction of smoke dispersion, thereby affecting evacuation time and passenger safety. Therefore,
firefighting route optimization is not only a key aspect of enhancing emergency response capabilities but also a crucial measure to
ensure the operational safety of subway stations.
Existing research primarily focuses on the phased analysis of fire development and the optimization of passenger evacuation
routes following a fire incident. For example, Zhang and Han [33] employed a CFD simulator to assess the effectiveness of a subway
platform smoke isolation system that combines air curtains with mechanical smoke extraction systems, demonstrating its capability
to control smoke spread and reduce temperatures under various fire conditions. Similarly, Liu and Zou [34] proposed an improved
ant colony algorithm for dynamically adjusting passenger evacuation routes within subway stations during fires, aiming to extend the
available safe evacuation time and enhance emergency evacuation outcomes. However, existing studies often overlook the potential
impact of firefighting strategies on passenger evacuation processes, which may lead to reduced evacuation efficiency and increased
safety risks. The lack of consideration for the coordinated optimization of firefighting strategies and evacuation procedures can result
in delayed fire control and incomplete emergency response strategies, thereby diminishing the effectiveness of emergency rescue
operations. Therefore, it is crucial to comprehensively consider the coordinated optimization of firefighting actions and evacuation
processes.
Firefighters’ route planning faces various uncertainties, particularly in travel time and firefighting duration, which may render the
planned optimal routes ineffective or suboptimal. Robust optimization theory [35] is a crucial method to address these challenges.
Uncertainty optimization methods mainly include stochastic programming, fuzzy programming, and robust optimization [36], each
offering diverse solutions based on the nature of the uncertainties and application scenarios. Stochastic programming treats uncertain
parameters as random variables with known probability distributions [37]. However, accurately obtaining these distributions during
emergencies is often impractical, limiting the applicability of this approach. Fuzzy programming represents uncertainty using fuzzy
sets but relies on prior knowledge of membership functions [38], introducing subjectivity and uncertainty in practical applications.

Robust optimization seeks solutions that remain feasible under all possible realizations of uncertain parameters within specified
bounds [39], emphasizing solution robustness. Yang et al. [40] proposed a robust evacuation path optimization model for subway
flood scenarios based on a box-intersection budget uncertainty set to enhance evacuation efficiency and decision robustness. Li et al.
[41] developed a two-stage robust optimization model based on a generalized budget uncertainty set for the location–allocation of
emergency service facilities, improving decision robustness and sustainability. In fire scenarios, uncertainties such as fire spread
rate, firefighting time, and passenger distribution are prevalent. Robust optimization can ensure that firefighting routes maintain
high reliability and adaptability across various fire situations.
3. The proposed model
This section proposes a robust optimization method for firefighting routes in subway station fire scenarios, as illustrated in Fig.
1. Initially, a fire evolution simulation system for the station is constructed using PyroSim software, while a corresponding passenger
evacuation system is developed with PathFinder software to obtain evacuation data during a fire incident. Subsequently, the black-
winged kite algorithm (BKA) is employed to optimize a gated recurrent unit (GRU) model for predicting passenger passage times at
nodes such as gates, providing data support for calculating firefighting travel time. Next, the uncertainties in travel and firefighting
time are incorporated into the construction of a robust optimization model for firefighting routes. To enhance solution convergence
speed, the model is solved by integrating Gurobi with the Ivy Algorithm (IVYA). Finally, the effectiveness of the firefighting routes
is analyzed using a comprehensive evaluation method for firefighting outcomes.
Assuming multiple fire outbreaks occur within a subway station, firefighting teams composed of staff members are deployed for
initial fire suppression. An upper-layer fire network is established, consisting of fire source points, while a lower-layer passenger
evacuation network is formed, incorporating critical evacuation nodes such as gates, stairs/escalators, and exits, as illustrated in
Fig. 2. The variables utilized in constructing the robust optimization model for firefighting routes are defined in Table 1.

When a fire occurs in a subway station, the complexity of the station’s structure, the dynamic spread of fire and smoke, and
the randomness of passenger flow in evacuations all contribute to significant uncertainties in the cost of route selection, firefighting
time, and firefighting effectiveness. Additionally, the high-temperature environment, low visibility, and potentially obstructed routes

The uncertainties in travel time and firefighting time stem from multiple factors, such as station structures, smoke diffusion, and
equipment operations, and also include dynamic fluctuations caused by the intersections of multiple firefighters, changes in passage
priorities, and the decay of firefighters’ combat effectiveness. This paper adopts box-intersection polyhedral uncertainty sets to
represent the uncertainties in the travel time and firefighting time of working firefighters [45]. The core idea is to mathematically
characterize the upper and lower bounds of individual parameters as well as the possible interaction constraints among these
parameters. These uncertainties are multidimensional and subject to upper and lower bounds. By adopting the form of box-
intersection polyhedral uncertainty sets, these uncertainties can be flexibly characterized, while the intersection form captures the
correlations between variables. This ensures robustness without being overly conservative.
The box-shaped uncertainty set shown in Eq. (18) is used to characterize the independent upper and lower bounds of each
parameter, directly representing the acceptable range of uncertain parameters under various fire conditions. Meanwhile, the
polyhedral uncertainty set shown in Eq. (19) captures the linear constraints and mutual correlations among parameters. By taking
the intersection of multiple box-shaped and polyhedral sets, the resulting box intersection polytope, as shown in Eq. (20), is
obtained. Compared with traditional interval or ellipsoidal uncertainty sets, this form can flexibly describe multi-dimensional
uncertain parameters with well-defined upper and lower bounds, while partially reflecting parameter correlations through its
intersection structure, thereby avoiding over-conservatism or excessive relaxation. Moreover, its linear constraint structure can be
easily embedded into the robust optimization model, allowing the solution to be formulated as a linear or convex optimization
problem, thus achieving a balance between modeling accuracy and computational efficiency.

3.2. The BKA-GRU node time prediction model
In calculating the travel time of firefighting personnel, nodes such as gates and stairs within the station may become bottlenecks,
affecting the movement speed of firefighters. A more precise understanding of the travel time of firefighting personnel can be
obtained by accurately calculating the passage time through these nodes [46]. This provides a reliable basis for optimizing
firefighting routes, ensuring the smooth execution of firefighting tasks while making the evacuation process efficient, ultimately
maximizing overall safety.
To meet the demands for calculating the passage time through nodes like gates and stairs, the BKA is introduced to optimize GRU.
GRU [47] partially addresses the gradient vanishing or exploding problems commonly faced by recurrent neural networks. It has a

Therefore, this paper employs GRU as the foundational model for predicting node passage time.

The key hyperparameters in the GRU model, such as dropout rate, typically require manual tuning. This process is not only
time-consuming but also overly reliant on experience, making it prone to local optima. Therefore, adjusting the hyperparameters
of the GRU model is crucial for enhancing its predictive performance. To address this, a novel BKA-GRU method is proposed to
automatically determine the optimal hyperparameter combination for the GRU model.
Two critical hyperparameters for the GRU model are the layer size and dropout rate. The layer size determines the learning and
representational capacities of the model. A larger layer size allows the model to capture more complex patterns but may lead to
overfitting, especially when the dataset is small. Conversely, a smaller layer size might cause underfitting, preventing the model from
capturing intricate patterns in the data. Besides, adjusting the dropout rate helps find the optimal level of regularization to reduce
the model’s reliance on training data. If the dropout rate is too high, the model’s learning capacity may be suppressed; if too low, it
may lead to overfitting. The optimal values for layer size and dropout rate are often not apparent and may include multiple potential
solutions. The proposed BKA-GRU model for node passage time prediction incorporates a BKA hyperparameter optimization module
into the GRU framework. This enables a global search to identify an ideal combination of layer size and dropout rate for the GRU,
effectively balancing model complexity and generalization capability.

The workflow of the BKA-GRU node passage time prediction model is illustrated in Fig. 3. Firstly, passage data collected at
subway station nodes, such as gates and stairs, is preprocessed and divided into training, validation, and testing sets. Next, the
BKA is used to optimize two key hyperparameters in the GRU model, namely hidden layer size and dropout rate, by continuously
searching for the optimal combination of these parameters to minimize the prediction error of the GRU-trained model. Subsequently,
the optimal hyperparameter combination generated by BKA, along with the training data, is fed into the GRU to train the node
passage time prediction model. Finally, the BKA-GRU model with accurate time prediction capability is obtained.
In the process of predicting gate passage time using the BKA-GRU model, factors such as passenger heterogeneity, passenger
quantity [50], gate width, and the number of gates are primarily used as model inputs [51]. For stair passage time prediction, inputs
mainly include passenger heterogeneity, passenger quantity, the number of stairs, stair types, stair lengths, and stair widths [52], as
shown in Fig. 4. The node passage time prediction model enhances the accuracy and speed of passage time calculations, providing
effective support for optimizing firefighting routes. Besides, the key variable of the number of passengers is introduced to capture the
congestion and traffic interference that occur when multiple firefighters and passengers traverse the same node, thereby reflecting
the resource competition between routes.

3.3. Comprehensive evaluation method for firefighting effect
In fire scenarios, optimizing firefighting routes within subway stations is a critical aspect of ensuring passenger safety and
efficient rescue operations. Passenger safety, as the core evaluation criterion during emergency firefighting and rescue efforts, is
significantly influenced by various factors. Firstly, exposure to high temperatures may result in severe burns or even fatalities.
Secondly, the accumulation of toxic gases poses substantial risks of poisoning or asphyxiation. Furthermore, fire-induced poor
visibility and crowding significantly increase the probability of secondary injuries, such as falls or collisions. These risks are
particularly severe in narrow subway passages or bottleneck areas like stairs, where large-scale casualties are more likely to occur. In
addition, crowding not only decreases evacuation efficiency but also extends firefighting routes, thereby compromising firefighting
effectiveness. It also interacts with factors such as the physical exertion of rescue personnel and the rate of fire spread, creating a
complex interdependence. To address these challenges, this study incorporates quantitative metrics of firefighter walking distance
𝐿∙, passenger safety risks 𝑅∙, and congestion density 𝐶∙, as illustrated in Fig. 5. A cost function, as expressed in Eq. (36), is
developed within a multidimensional evaluation framework to systematically assess the dynamic relationship between firefighting
route optimization and passenger evacuation effectiveness [53].

4. Solving method for robust optimization model
4.1. Peer model conversion
To effectively solve the robust optimization model for firefighting routes shown in Eq. (7), it is necessary to transform the
infinite constraints in the original model into finite constraints, thereby converting the semi-infinite programming problem into
an optimization problem with explicitly convex constraints. By introducing appropriate variables or approximating uncertainties,
the problem can be reformulated into a finite number of convex constraints [54], preserving the robustness of the solution while
avoiding the computational difficulties associated with directly solving a semi-infinite programming problem. In this paper, the
box-intersection polytope is chosen as the uncertainty set primarily due to its simple mathematical structure and convexity, which
make the robust optimization problem solvable and easy to handle. This uncertainty set effectively captures the uncertainties in both
the travel time of firefighting personnel and the firefighting time. The use of the box intersection polytope enables the transformation
of the original model into an optimization problem with finite constraints, ensuring both solvability and computational efficiency.
This provides a rational solution for developing optimal firefighting routes that exhibit robust characteristics.

4.2. Model solving
After the equivalent transformation, the robust optimization model for the firefighting routes of subway station staff, as shown in
Eq. (58), can be solved by invoking the Gurobi [57] solver. However, due to the high complexity of the transformations involving
the firefighting route decision variables, the computation process may require a significant amount of time. To further improve
the solution efficiency, this paper leverages the strengths of IVYA [58] both global search and local optimization, integrating it
with Gurobi to solve the optimization model. This optimized solving strategy enables the model to converge quickly to the global
optimum, ensuring that the firefighting route plans exhibit greater robustness and effectively adapt to various uncertainties in the
environment.

5. Case study
To verify the effectiveness of the firefighting route optimization model proposed in this paper, we conducted a simulation study
using Liaoyang East Road Subway Station in Qingdao, China, as the case study scenario. Qingdao Liaoyang East Road Station
was selected as a case study based on the following considerations. First, as a transfer station with high passenger flow and typical
passenger characteristics, it can effectively reflect the requirements for firefighting route optimization under complex passenger flow
conditions. In addition, the station’s intricate structure, including multiple platforms and stairways, allows the model’s applicability
and effectiveness to be fully demonstrated under complex multi-node, multi-channel, and multi-path conditions. Choosing this station
as a validation case highlights the model’s generalizability and practical value in the complex subway station scenarios.
During the simulation process, PyroSim software was used to simulate the fire evolution trends in the subway station, while
Pathfinder software was employed to simulate passenger evacuation behavior under fire conditions. The station structure and fire
point locations are shown in Fig. 7. The fire simulation in this study follows the requirements of the Standard for fire protection
design of metro (GB 51298-2018). Smoke-resistant zoning was implemented within the station interior, and mechanical smoke
exhaust and supply air systems were installed in each smoke-resistant zone. Smoke control and air supply between the platform and
hall levels were achieved by calculating the required mechanical exhaust and supply air volumes. This design effectively maintains
pressure differentials within the smoke-resistant zones, limiting smoke diffusion and ensuring the rationality and reliability of the
PyroSim simulation results under fire scenarios. Since this study focuses on firefighting and rescue scenarios in subway stations and
does not involve train operations, the platform screen doors remain closed throughout the simulation, and it is assumed that trains
will not stop at the station in the event of a fire.

the variation patterns of passage time, demonstrating strong predictive capability and generalization performance. It can provide
high-precision time parameter support in complex emergency scenarios.

colony optimization (ACO) as alternative algorithms to IVYA for comparative analysis of the model-solving results. This study adopts
the convergence time benchmark test method, using a preset fitness threshold to quantitatively compare the convergence speeds

solving speeds for different algorithms in addressing the same problem are quantified and compared, thereby providing a scientific

of firefighting strategies. It employs the BKA optimization algorithm to enhance the GRU algorithm for calculating passage time at

This study proposes a robust optimization method for firefighting routes in subway station fire scenarios. However, certain
limitations remain. First, the model requires real-time computation during the solution process. Although computational efficiency
has been substantially improved through the introduction of an enhanced algorithm structure and robust constraint design, its
online responsiveness in extremely complex scenarios still requires further optimization. Second, the experimental validation was
primarily conducted in a simulation environment. While the structure and fire evolution parameters of an actual subway station
were referenced, a gap remains between the model and real-world fire emergency scenarios. Moreover, the model’s performance
depends to some extent on the completeness and accuracy of input data, and real-time data collection in fire environments
remains subject to uncertainty. Future research could further assess and refine the model’s dynamic adaptability and stability by
integrating data from field emergency drills or multi-source sensor information. Additionally, exploring dynamic update methods
that combine multi-source heterogeneous data with online learning mechanisms to enhance the model’s predictive accuracy and
generalization capability under complex and uncertain conditions represents a promising direction for future work. Furthermore,
explicitly incorporating the dynamic decline in firefighters’ operational effectiveness could improve the model’s practical relevance
and predictive accuracy.
