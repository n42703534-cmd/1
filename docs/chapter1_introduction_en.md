# 1 Introduction

## 1.1 Background and motivation

By the end of 2024, 61 cities in mainland China had opened urban rail transit lines with a total operating mileage exceeding 11 000 km, of which metro lines account for more than 75%[1]. While the continuously expanding network provides convenience for millions of citizens, it also turns the inherent features of underground metro stations—enclosed space, multi-level layout, long transfer passages, and highly asymmetric exit capacities—into serious evacuation risks during emergencies. Accident records from the past decade confirm these concerns: in April 2015 a passenger fainted on the platform of Huangbeiling Station (Shenzhen Metro Line 5), triggering a panic stampede that injured 12 people; in September 2017 an explosion occurred at Parsons Green Station in London, injuring at least 29; in July 2021, Train 04,502 of Zhengzhou Metro Line 5 was flooded and lost power, and although 953 people were eventually rescued, 14 died [2–4]. Floods have been identified as the third most frequent type of disaster in metro stations, accounting for approximately 11% of recorded accidents [4]. These events repeatedly demonstrate that correct and efficient evacuation route guidance is a decisive factor in protecting passengers’ lives. Consequently, research on evacuation route planning for multi-line transfer stations under different passenger loads carries both engineering safety value and fundamental significance for pedestrian dynamics.

Research on pedestrian evacuation dynamics is generally divided into two broad categories: empirical studies and model studies [5]. Model studies can be further partitioned into planning models and simulation models. Planning models employ Dijkstra's algorithm [6], A* and its variants [7–8], ant colony optimisation [9], game theory and machine‑learning‑enhanced methods [3] to generate optimal or near‑optimal evacuation route sets on a given network topology and passenger distribution. Simulation models, on the other hand, rely on the social‑force model [10–11], cellular‑automaton models [12] and commercial platforms such as PathFinder, Anylogic and MassMotion to simulate the spatio‑temporal evolution of crowds, identify bottlenecks and predict evacuation times [13–15]. Owing to safety risks and operational constraints, large‑scale, high‑density, full‑size evacuation experiments are rarely feasible in real metro stations—every existing empirical method has significant limitations regarding the specificity of underground station space [2]—so simulation remains the dominant research tool in this field. Nevertheless, the accuracy of both planning and simulation models depends critically on the empirical calibration of model parameters; simulation results that lack experimental validation can deviate substantially from real scenarios in terms of both evacuation time and bottleneck distribution [2]. Against this background, breaking through the closed‑loop validation of a single simulation toolchain and incorporating an independent external simulation benchmark into the evaluation framework of evacuation strategies is an urgent direction for progress in this domain.

Within the metro‑station context, passenger evacuation route optimisation involves three tightly coupled sub‑problems: quantitative modelling of facility capacity (gates, stairs and escalators), mathematical formulation of the route‑planning objective, and on‑line execution and verification of the plan. With respect to facility capacity, ticket gates are the critical control nodes connecting the paid and unpaid zones, and their throughput is jointly influenced by the number of gates, the effective width of each passage and the arrival pattern of passenger flows [16–17]. Notably, Li et al. [2] observed in a full‑size, on‑site experiment at Wulichong Station (Guiyang) that passengers’ gate‑channel choice depended on their final exit decision rather than on the channel width per se. This experimental finding has an important implication for route planning: if, at the behavioural level, passengers have already bound their gate‑channel choice to their exit decision, then shifting capacity awareness from the individual‑facility level forward to the exit‑evaluation level—i.e., adjusting the ranking of candidate exit directions according to the gate capacity associated with each exit, already during the route‑search phase—is a more behaviourally coherent and, in engineering terms, more direct intervention.

## 1.2 State of the art and gaps

Route‑planning methods can be classified into two paradigms. The first is **static route optimisation**: a complete path is planned once before evacuation begins. Representative approaches include single‑objective models that minimise total evacuation time [18] and multi‑objective models that simultaneously consider evacuation time, congestion and risk [10,19], typically solved via NSGA‑II to obtain a Pareto‑optimal set. The second is **dynamic per‑hop guidance**: instead of fixing a complete path in advance, the next‑hop direction is selected at each decision step based on the real‑time congestion state of the current node and its downstream neighbours [20–21]. Compared with static optimisation, dynamic guidance offers better responsiveness to time‑varying congestion and greater resilience to local network changes caused by facility failures. Nevertheless, both paradigms share two important limitations.

First, **exit‑capacity information is perceived passively rather than embedded proactively**. Existing congestion metrics usually use the cumulative number of users on an edge or node as a proxy [22] and do not explicitly differentiate the throughput differences among gate groups within the same station. In dynamic guidance methods, capacity information is implicitly contained in the queue‑waiting term and becomes visible in the cost function only after a node has already accumulated a large queue—by which time the weaker exit is already congested and any further re‑routing yields diminishing returns because of severe upstream back‑pressure. When the estimated travel times of two paths are similar but the gate‑group capacities at their respective exits differ by a factor of several (e.g., at Longyang Road Station the four exits of the L2 concourse have throughputs ranging from about 2.08 pers/s to 4.86 pers/s), neither static nor dynamic methods can proactively avoid directing excessive passenger flow toward the weaker exit in the decision‑making stage, causing local bottlenecks to persistently worsen under high‑load scenarios.

Second, **effectiveness evaluation has mostly been conducted within a single simulation toolchain**—the optimised routes are solved in a mathematical model and then injected into the simulation model within the same software environment for comparison [10,19,23–24]. Such “pre‑optimisation vs. post‑optimisation” comparisons lack systematic cross‑validation against an independent external simulation benchmark: the conclusions may be biased by the default behaviour mode built into a particular software package (e.g., the Steering or SFPE modes of PathFinder) [2] and are difficult for other researchers to independently reproduce and for practitioners to adopt. The Goto Any Exit behaviour mode of PathFinder represents purely unguided pedestrian behaviour based on spatial proximity and dynamic density perception; using it as an independent external benchmark provides an evaluation criterion that is far more convincing and reproducible than an internal before‑and‑after comparison.

## 1.3 This work

To address the above gaps, this paper takes Longyang Road Station, a five‑line transfer hub in Shanghai, as the case study, constructs a high‑fidelity graph‑based station evacuation simulation model, and proposes an **exit‑capacity‑aware adaptive single‑hop guidance algorithm—Adaptive Queue‑Aware A\* (AA\*)**.

Longyang Road Station sits in the Shanghai rail transit network and interchanges Lines L2, L7, L16, L18 and the Maglev. The vertical offset between platforms exceeds 30 m (the L18 platform is at −16.97 m and the L16 platform at +12.53 m); the concourses are connected via long transfer corridors, escalator groups and a skybridge, making the station a typical deep‑elevated mixed transfer hub. The station has 16 ground exits in total. Among them, the L2 concourse possesses EXIT 2 (four wide gates, μ ≈ 2.78 pers/s), EXIT 3 (five wide gates, μ ≈ 3.47 pers/s), EXIT 4 (seven wide gates, μ ≈ 4.86 pers/s) and EXIT 6 (three wide gates, μ ≈ 2.08 pers/s); the maximum throughput ratio is about 2.3 ×. This constitutes a prototypical asymmetric‑exit configuration and provides a natural experimental setting for examining the capacity‑awareness capability of dynamic route guidance.

The core design of AA\* is as follows. At each simulation time step, the time‑varying cost of every network edge is first computed as $g = l/v(\rho) + Q/\mu$, where the first term is the density‑dependent traversal time (Fruin speed–density model) and the second is the queue‑waiting time at the target bottleneck node. A\* search then yields the minimum‑cost path to each candidate exit. Afterwards, AA\* performs a **capacity‑sensitive ranking** of the candidate exit set: for each candidate path the algorithm extracts the throughput $\mu_k$ of the last service‑bottleneck node (typically the ticket gate) along the path, and discounts the raw path cost $c_j$ according to the equivalent‑cost formula $\tilde{c}_j = c_j / \sqrt{\mu_k}$. This capacity‑weighting term is independent of the instantaneous queue variable $Q$; it can give a ranking advantage to paths leading to wider gates even when queues are empty, thereby achieving proactive capacity‑aware flow splitting from the very first evacuation step. At the same time, AA\* employs an inertia (hysteresis) mechanism that triggers a path switch only when the cost improvement exceeds a preset threshold, suppressing unstable oscillations caused by minor cost fluctuations and preserving the temporal continuity of the guidance signal. The output of the algorithm is a single next‑hop direction for each active node; pedestrian flow then propagates along edges in a **strictly single‑path manner**, without introducing any facility‑level parallel splitting or mandatory exit assignment, thereby keeping the boundary between the route‑decision layer and the physical‑execution layer clean.

The contributions of this paper are threefold:

**(1) Embedding bottleneck throughput directly into the path‑ranking layer.** Through the discount formula $\tilde{c}_j = c_j / \sqrt{\mu_k}$, capacity awareness takes effect even when queues are empty. The proactive biasing during the sparse‑flow stage effectively reduces the formation of large queues in front of weak exits during the subsequent high‑load stage, forming a two‑phase “widen‑first,‑relieve‑later” strategy.

**(2) Conducting a systematic two‑scenario evaluation with PathFinder as the independent external benchmark.** Under regular emergency conditions (mode 1, 2187 persons) and bidirectional full‑train load conditions (mode 4, 17 905 persons), AA\* is compared against Improved A\* [20] on total evacuation time, cumulative queuing time (person·s), moderate and severe congestion exposure (person·s), and exit‑utilisation evenness (HHI). Facility‑by‑facility path tracing (platform waiting zone → stairs/escalator → gate → exit) quantitatively reveals how the capacity‑aware mechanism regulates the exit‑choice distribution of different passenger‑source groups and the physical mechanism by which it relieves congestion at weak exits under high load.

**(3) Establishing a clear boundary between the execution model and the route‑planning model.** By explicitly isolating non‑routing execution‑layer effects such as parallel‑facility correction and spillback, the causal relationship between AA\*'s route decisions and the underlying flow propagation can be independently analysed, quantitatively attributed and parametrically calibrated, providing a clean modular framework for subsequent model improvement and engineering deployment.

## 1.4 Paper structure

Section 2 presents the station evacuation network modelling method, including node and edge definitions, facility throughput parameters and the granular representation of platform waiting zones. Section 3 details the design of the AA\* algorithm, covering the edge‑cost function, the capacity‑aware exit ranking and the inertia‑based stabilisation mechanism. Section 4 describes the experimental setup and reports the multi‑metric comparison results under the mode 1 and mode 4 scenarios. Section 5 carries out congestion tracing and capacity‑awareness mechanism analysis, quantifying the spatial paths via which AA\* adjusts exit distributions. Section 6 discusses the applicability conditions and limitations of the method. Section 7 concludes the paper.
